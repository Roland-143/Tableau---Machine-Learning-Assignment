# Regional and Pandemic Analysis

## Scope and method

This analysis uses the project’s 16 selected states—four each from the Northeast, Midwest, South, and West—and annual BEA Real GDP reported through FRED for 2016–2025. Values are in millions of chained 2017 dollars. The source already includes a Census-style `Region` field, and its state assignments agree with the project state list. State-level year-over-year (YoY) growth is calculated within each state as `(current-year GDP / prior-year GDP − 1) × 100`; the 2016 value is blank because the series begins in 2016. Regional figures below are sums of the four selected states’ Real GDP values, with growth calculated from consecutive sums. They are not estimates of the complete Census regions.

The period labels are analytical groupings: pre-pandemic (2016–2019), pandemic shock (2019–2020 change), initial recovery (2020–2021 change), and post-pandemic observations (2022–2025). They describe timing, not causation.

## 1. Regional GDP Trends

**Observation.** The selected-state GDP totals increased over the study window in all four regions, but both their scale and growth paths differed.

**Evidence.** From 2016 to 2025, combined GDP rose from $3.44 trillion to $4.04 trillion in the Northeast (+17.19%); $2.31 trillion to $2.68 trillion in the Midwest (+16.05%); $3.71 trillion to $5.11 trillion in the South (+37.81%); and $3.78 trillion to $5.05 trillion in the West (+33.72%). These values are sums for the four selected states in each region.

**Interpretation.** The South had the largest percentage increase over the full period and the highest 2025 selected-state total. The West also grew substantially, while the Northeast and Midwest had smaller cumulative gains.

**Why it matters.** The trend view distinguishes changes in aggregate economic scale from growth rates. Because it covers only four sampled states per region, it should not be described as a ranking of complete Census-region economies.

## 2. Economic Impact of the 2020 Pandemic

**Observation.** Fifteen of the 16 states had lower Real GDP in 2020 than in 2019; Arizona was the exception.

**Evidence.** Illinois had the largest decline, −4.96%, followed by Pennsylvania (−4.10%) and Michigan (−3.61%). Washington had the smallest decline among states that contracted (−0.41%). Arizona increased by 1.81%. The regional sums fell most in the Midwest (−3.60%), followed by the Northeast (−2.84%), South (−1.36%), and West (−0.84%).

**Interpretation.** The shock was uneven across the sample. Illinois and Pennsylvania were especially affected by the measured decline, while the West’s selected-state aggregate contracted least; Arizona’s positive result is a state-level outlier relative to the other 15 states.

**Why it matters.** The state bar chart identifies variation hidden by a single national or regional average. These are observed changes in Real GDP, not evidence that the pandemic alone caused each state’s outcome.

## 3. Initial Economic Recovery

**Observation.** Every selected state recorded positive year-over-year Real GDP growth in 2021, but the pace varied.

**Evidence.** Florida had the strongest increase (+9.37%), followed by Arizona (+8.70%); Pennsylvania had the weakest (+3.57%). The regional sums grew 7.80% in the West, 7.71% in the South, 6.10% in the Midwest, and 4.83% in the Northeast. Pennsylvania’s 2021 GDP remained 0.68% below its 2019 value, even though it grew in 2021.

**Interpretation.** The largest 2020 decline did not consistently predict the strongest rebound. Illinois, hardest hit in 2020, grew 6.61% in 2021—not the strongest recovery—while Florida had the highest 2021 growth after a smaller 2020 decline of 1.25%. Pennsylvania combined the second-largest 2020 contraction with the weakest 2021 growth.

**Why it matters.** Putting the two aligned bar charts side by side makes resilience and rebound distinct concepts. Positive annual growth does not necessarily mean that a state had returned to its pre-shock GDP level by 2021.

## 4. Post-Pandemic Regional Trends and Growth Heat Map

**Observation.** The 2021 rebound was followed by different regional growth patterns in 2022–2025; post-pandemic average growth did not simply reproduce the pre-pandemic ordering.

**Evidence.** Comparing mean annual growth over 2017–2019 with 2022–2025, the South moved from 3.21% to 4.23%, the Midwest from 1.57% to 2.01%, and the Northeast was nearly unchanged (2.06% to 2.00%). The West slowed from 4.43% to 2.38%. All four selected-state regional sums were above their 2019 totals by 2021, but Pennsylvania’s state GDP was still below its own 2019 level in 2021. In the heat map, negative 2020 cells are most pronounced for Illinois, Pennsylvania, and Michigan; positive 2021 cells show the broad rebound.

**Interpretation.** Recovery was not a uniform return to the earlier growth pattern: South-region selected-state growth strengthened on this comparison, while West-region growth moderated. The heat map adds state-level heterogeneity that regional lines conceal.

**Why it matters.** Showing annual rates across the full time span places the shock and rebound in context and prevents one recovery year from being mistaken for a long-term trend. The post-pandemic period here is only four observations (2022–2025).

## 5. Key Findings

1. The selected states in the South had the strongest 2016–2025 aggregate percentage increase (+37.81%); the Northeast had the weakest (+17.19%).
2. The Midwest’s selected-state sum had the largest 2020 contraction (−3.60%), while the West contracted least (−0.84%).
3. Illinois had the largest state decline in 2020 (−4.96%); Arizona was the only state with positive growth (+1.81%).
4. Florida led 2021 state growth (+9.37%), while Pennsylvania lagged (+3.57%) and remained below its 2019 GDP level.
5. Regional average growth patterns differed after 2021: the South strengthened and the West slowed relative to their respective 2017–2019 averages.

## Limitations

The sample contains 16 states, not every state in each Census region; its regional aggregates are custom sums of selected-state series, not official BEA regional totals. Real GDP measures production, not household income or well-being, and this descriptive comparison does not control for industry mix, population, or other factors. FRED’s BEA-based historical observations may be revised.
